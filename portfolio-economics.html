Peer-reviewed articles

  • "Financial Regulations and Price Inconsistencies across Bitcoin Markets," with Sofia Vivanco. Information Economics and Policy.

    Every bitcoin is identical, so a lasting price gap between markets must come from the markets themselves. Across 11 exchanges, together 26% of global bitcoin trade volume, we find that exchanges that do not require customer identification to open an account are more likely to deviate from representative market prices, so know-your-customer rules can have a non-negligible effect on the bitcoin market.

    Data. Daily bitcoin prices in US dollars from 11 exchanges, together 26% of global bitcoin trade volume, and a bitcoin price index, June 2014 to July 2015, with 22 characteristics of each exchange collected by hand from its own pages, such as whether it requires identification to fund an account.

    Methods. Unit-root tests: DF-GLS, KPSS. Cointegration tests of the law of one price: Johansen trace, Pesaran-Shin-Smith bounds. Toda-Yamamoto Granger causality. OLS with robust standard errors and date fixed effects, regressing each exchange's price deviation on its characteristics.

    Read the paper

Working papers/work in progress

  • "Bitcoin Reveals Exchange Rate Manipulation and Detects Capital Controls"

    The data used to detect currency manipulation and capital controls are low-frequency, costly, lagged, and possibly mismeasured. I show that bitcoin prices, once adjusted for bitcoin-market-specific effects, approximate unofficial exchange rates at a daily frequency, and so reveal both whether a distortion exists and how large it is, including short interventions that would otherwise go undetected. The result also confirms that bitcoin is used to get around capital controls.

    Data. Daily transaction-weighted bitcoin prices for 17 currencies on four exchanges, June 2014 to September 2015, with official exchange rates.

    Methods. Bitcoin-implied exchange rates, adjusted for bitcoin-market trends using the euro as the benchmark. Cointegration tests of each bitcoin rate against the official rate, Johansen trace and Pesaran-Shin-Smith bounds, to detect capital controls. Toda-Yamamoto Granger causality. Validation against the Chinn-Ito capital-openness index and the IMF's exchange-rate regime classification.

    Read the paper

  • "The Composition of the Allocation Puzzle through Time"

    The allocation puzzle is the positive correlation between growth and capital outflows across developing countries. I split capital outflows into private savings, public debt, and international reserve flows, and follow each from the start of a growth episode. The private-savings correlation leads the start of growth, the public and reserve correlations lag it, and the correlation for total outflows disappears after twenty years. None of the proposed solutions matches these findings.

    Data. Net foreign assets from Lane and Milesi-Ferretti, converted to constant international dollars with Penn World Table prices; international reserves from the IMF and public debt from the World Development Indicators; productivity built from the Penn World Table; the Chinn-Ito openness index and foreign aid as controls. 36 countries with an identified growth start and twenty years of data.

    Methods. A modified growth-acceleration rule, after Hausmann and co-authors, dates the start of each productivity growth episode. Cross-sectional regressions of each capital-flow component on productivity growth, year by year, from five years before the start to twenty years after.

  • "Re-evaluating Stylized Business Cycle Facts for Developed and Developing Countries"

    Business cycle statistics are usually computed on cycles of two to eight years, a window set by Burns and Mitchell from US data. I date business cycles country by country and find longer cycles, 35 to 66 quarters for developed countries and 28 to 59 for developing ones. Re-estimating the stylized facts on these windows narrows the volatility gap between developed and developing countries and changes the cyclicality of government spending.

    Data. Quarterly real GDP from the OECD, with consumption, government spending, imports, and exports, for the United States and other countries including Korea, Mexico, and South Africa; 1985 to 2011 (Mexico from 1993).

    Methods. A simplified Bry-Boschan turning-point procedure, checked against NBER dates for the United States. Christiano-Fitzgerald band-pass filtering on each country's own cycle window, then volatility, correlation, and relative standard deviations.

  • "The Effect of Income Inequality on Import Prices"

    Earlier studies find that higher income inequality in an importing country lowers the unit value of its imports, and changes both the value of trade and the mark-up on identical goods, in either direction. I build a monopolistic-competition model of trade with income-based price discrimination that reproduces all three findings. Each consumer chooses the variety to buy, so high inequality leads richer consumers to buy more varieties at higher prices and poorer consumers fewer varieties at lower prices. Simulated, it captures about half of the observed link between inequality and trade value.

    Data. Product-level US exports to all trading partners from UN Comtrade; population, GDP per capita, and the income share of the top 40% from the World Development Indicators; distance, borders, and language from CEPII; the Rauch index to pick differentiated goods. 55 countries.

    Methods. A heterogeneous-firm trade model with non-homothetic preferences, calibrated by minimum distance over a grid of resale-constraint values. Trade barriers estimated by gravity from trade shares. Log-linear regressions of trade value, quantity, and unit value on inequality, with product fixed effects and errors clustered by destination.

Methods and tools

Models and computational simulation

  • Computational modeling: models solved and simulated numerically. The macroeconomic models are micro-founded: dynamic general equilibrium models in which households and firms optimize, solved for steady states and transition paths. Techniques include:
    • Policy simulation in general equilibrium: dynamic general equilibrium models, built and simulated in MATLAB, that trace a policy change through interest rates, exchange rates, and the rest of the economy. Used in: a client's central bank digital currency project, on the effects of CBDC adoption on interest rates and exchange rates.
    • Programming macroeconomic models: dynamic general equilibrium models programmed in MATLAB, Dynare, or Python: consumption and savings with the Euler equation; Solow and neoclassical growth; real business cycles and international real business cycles; and cash-in-advance, money-in-utility, overlapping-generations, and Baumol-Tobin models of money, with seigniorage. Used in: The Elements of Economic Analysis III; Economic Policy Analysis.
    • Policy experiments with steady states and transition paths: lump-sum and proportional taxation, optimal taxation and government debt, social security, unemployment insurance, seigniorage, and the central bank's commitment problem, solved in MATLAB. Used in: Economic Policy Analysis.
    • Heterogeneous-firm models and calibration: a Melitz trade model with non-homothetic preferences, Pareto productivity, and income-based price discrimination, solved, simulated, and calibrated by minimum distance over a grid of parameter values, for 55 countries. Used in: The Effect of Income Inequality on Import Prices.
    • Bayesian learning and dynamic programming: a growth model of a small open economy, built in MATLAB: beliefs about the components of TFP updated by a Kalman filter, TFP components estimated by GMM, and the model solved by value-function iteration on belief grids. Used in: "Learning about Growth" (second place, Hardy Third Year Paper Competition, University of Minnesota).
  • Stylized models: simplified models with closed-form solutions, to identify the channels through which a policy or a shock changes an outcome. Set as homework, practice, or exam problems in: Principles of Macroeconomics; The Elements of Economic Analysis I; The Elements of Economic Analysis II; The Elements of Economic Analysis III; International Monetary Systems; Economic Policy Analysis; International Trade; Macroeconomics; Risk and Benefits of Digitized Money and Assets. Past applications include:
    • Output, money, and growth: aggregate demand and aggregate supply; the money market; the Solow-Swan model and growth accounting.
    • Consumers, firms, and markets: consumer theory, from utility maximization and the Slutsky decomposition to expected utility; producer theory; general equilibrium of endowment and production economies; and Cournot, Bertrand, and Stackelberg oligopoly.
    • Payments and money demand: cash versus cards in a Baumol-Tobin model; a fall in money demand traced through prices, interest rates, and the exchange rate; a shift from the bolívar to bitcoin in Venezuela; a money-in-utility model with CES preferences.
    • Exchange rates and capital flows: interest parity with crypto as a currency converter; triangular arbitrage among the Turkish lira, Tether, and the US dollar; purchasing-power parity in where bitcoin mining locates; the trilemma as digital money weakens capital controls.
    • Exchange rates and the balance of payments: balance-of-payments accounting; spot and forward exchange rates and the returns on domestic and foreign assets; interest parity and the asset-market model of the exchange rate; the current account; real exchange rates and purchasing-power parity; the path of the nominal exchange rate after permanent and temporary changes in inflation and the money supply; pegs, capital flight, and a country's net wealth by currency.
    • Runs and taxes: a Diamond-Dybvig run at FTX; runs and deposit insurance; tax incidence on crypto miners.
    • International trade: the Ricardian, specific-factors, and Heckscher-Ohlin models; free movement of labor; economies of scale and monopolistic competition; and trade barriers, from tariffs to subsidies and dumping.

Data and measurement

Data construction and validation

  • Reconciling prices across sources: daily bitcoin prices from 11 exchanges, compared with a reference price to measure each exchange's deviation. Used in: Financial Regulations....
  • Collecting what no database holds: 22 characteristics of each exchange, such as its customer-identification rules, gathered by hand from the exchanges' own pages (used in Financial Regulations...), and one day's trading volume for every pairing, on every exchange, of the 50 largest cryptocurrencies.
  • Matching to official benchmarks: bitcoin prices in 52 currencies matched to official exchange rates from the IMF, BIS, FRED, and a commercial database, with 33 currencies kept.
  • Validating a new measure: bitcoin-implied exchange rates in 17 currencies, checked against the Chinn-Ito capital-openness index and the IMF's exchange-rate regime classification. Used in: Bitcoin Reveals....
  • Building productivity and capital-flow series: total factor productivity from output, labor, and a constructed capital stock, and net foreign assets split into private savings, public debt, and reserve flows. Used in: The Composition of the Allocation Puzzle through Time.
  • Building capital stocks: growth accounting with perpetual-inventory capital stocks, using Caselli-Feyrer initial stocks and Penn World Table data. Used in: The Composition of the Allocation Puzzle through Time.
  • Dating growth accelerations: a modified Hausmann et al. rule, 77 episodes in 36 countries. Used in: The Composition of the Allocation Puzzle through Time.
  • Dating business cycles from quarterly output: turning points found country by country and checked against NBER dates. Used in: Re-evaluating Stylized Business Cycle Facts for Developed and Developing Countries.
  • Merging trade, distance, and income-distribution data: product-level trade with distance, language, and income-share data, after dropping extreme unit values and products sold to fewer than five destinations, with the Rauch and BEC classifications merged. Used in: The Effect of Income Inequality on Import Prices.
  • Classifying job postings: 9,600 Job Openings for Economists postings classified by keyword in Stata, and a representative economics department built from the AEA's Universal Academic Questionnaire tables, with Christopher J. Roark. Used in: Seeking Teachers; The Job Market for Non-Tenure Track Academic Economists.
  • Working across standard sources: Penn World Table, World Development Indicators, OECD, IMF, the external-wealth positions of Lane and Milesi-Ferretti, product-level trade from UN Comtrade with CEPII distance and language data, and exchange-level and aggregated cryptoasset prices.

Econometrics

  • Unit-root tests (do shocks fade?): DF-GLS, augmented Dickey-Fuller, Phillips-Perron, KPSS. Used in: Financial Regulations... (DF-GLS, KPSS); Bitcoin Reveals... (augmented Dickey-Fuller, KPSS).
  • Cointegration and error-correction models (do series move together?): Johansen trace, Pesaran-Shin-Smith bounds, VECM. Used in: Financial Regulations...; Bitcoin Reveals....
  • Granger causality (which series is a leading signal?): Toda-Yamamoto, from a vector autoregression. Used in: Financial Regulations...; Bitcoin Reveals....
  • Fixed-effects regression (what explains differences across series?): OLS with fixed effects and robust standard errors. Used in: Financial Regulations....
  • Band-pass filtering (how long is a business cycle?): Christiano-Fitzgerald, on country-specific cycle lengths. Used in: Re-evaluating Stylized Business Cycle Facts for Developed and Developing Countries.
  • Gravity regressions (what drives trade values and prices?): trade value, quantity, and unit value, with product fixed effects and errors clustered by destination; Eaton-Kortum estimation of trade barriers. Used in: The Effect of Income Inequality on Import Prices.
  • Event-time regressions (what changes around a growth episode?): cross-sectional regressions around growth episodes, in eight specifications. Used in: The Composition of the Allocation Puzzle through Time.
  • Random-walk tests (do prices follow a random walk?): Jarque-Bera, Box-Pierce portmanteau, runs, and variance-ratio tests.
