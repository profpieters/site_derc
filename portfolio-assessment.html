Teaching and evaluation

What I do

I teach economics, and I create and evaluate problems: I write the problems and answer keys that test whether students can do economics, grade and review the answers, and referee, select, and edit economic work. Below, to help you judge whether I fit your project: my selected work in this field, the methods and tools I would bring to it, my advising and outreach, and my review and teaching. Selected work

Academic journals, chapters, and policy writing

Peer-reviewed articles

  • "Measuring Academic Mindset in Economics Courses," with 25 co-authors. International Review of Economics Education, June 2026.

    Proposes an aggregate measure of academic mindset, covering relevance, belonging, and growth, and examines how students' demographic characteristics predict their mindset when they enter an economics course. Students who do not identify as male, and first-generation college students, start with a lower mindset, and the difference is larger in introductory courses than in upper-level courses.

    Data. 3,212 students in 45 courses taught by 35 instructors at 24 U.S. institutions.

    Read the paper

  • "Designing Effective Assessments in Economics Courses: Guiding Principles," The Journal of Economic Education, 2024.

    For new instructors: what pedagogical theory says about sound assessment design, how it applies to economics courses, and examples of assessments from the economics literature to try.

    Read the paper

Newsletters

  • "The Job Market for Non-Tenure Track Academic Economists," with Chris Roark. CSWEP News, Issue II, 2022.

    Using the AEA's Universal Academic Questionnaire and its Job Openings for Economists postings, we show how the composition of economics departments has changed and how demand for non-tenure-track faculty has grown since the 2008 financial crisis.

Methods and tools

Creating problems

  • Homework and exams: designed all homework and exams for every course I taught, from principles to Ph.D.; in the intermediate theory courses and International Monetary Systems, homework tests analysis rather than recall. Principles set out in: Designing Effective Assessments in Economics Courses.
  • Answer keys: wrote the answer keys for my homework, quizzes, and exams.
  • Grading rubrics: developed grading rubrics for consistent weekly homework and exam grading across as many as nine teaching assistants.
  • Parallel versions: wrote back-up versions of the quizzes for every course I taught at the University of Chicago.
  • Problems built on models: problems that apply simplified economic models to real cases, such as a run at FTX or a shift from the bolívar to bitcoin, set in nine courses; the models are listed under Economic analysis.
  • Writing and data assignments: policy-brief prompts, one on a policy of the student's choosing (Economic Policy Analysis); a 15-page research paper built through weekly drafts (International Trade); five data projects, including a trading strategy written as code to run on an exchange (Cryptoasset Markets).
  • Practice problems: topic summaries and practice problems written for the course. Used in: Principles of Macroeconomics; International Monetary Systems.

Designing measures

  • Assessment designed around how students' thinking develops: the Perry and WWK models, five levels of undergraduate critical thinking from silence to constructed knowledge, applied to assessment design in: Designing Effective Assessments in Economics Courses.
  • Choosing how to assess: assessment method and frequency chosen for each course; assessments designed to work in person and online; course assessment and delivery redesigned for COVID-era teaching.
  • Grading schemes: each designed to fit its students, course, and institution, for example a grade taken from whichever of several weighting plans gives the student the highest grade, only the best scores counted, make-up quizzes and tests open to every student, drafts that set a floor for the paper grade, and grade cutoffs that a curve can lower but never raise.

Teaching methods and tools

  • Discussion and peer learning: the Avalanche method and in-class quick-response exercises, in classes of any size; students posted their code and data to a shared folder and learned from each other's work (Cryptoasset Markets).
  • Learning by computing: students solved and simulated models in MATLAB and Dynare (Intermediate Macroeconomics) or in MATLAB or Python (Economic Policy Analysis), and pulled exchange data through APIs in Python (Cryptoasset Markets).
  • Learning from public data: students worked with FRED series and graphs; graphed and regressed World Bank, Gapminder, and College Scorecard data in Excel; and did group problem sets on open data repositories. Used in: Principles of Macroeconomics; International Monetary Systems; Intermediate Macroeconomics; Economic Policy Analysis; Macroeconomics.
  • Disclosed AI use: students could use AI tools if they disclosed the tool and the prompt (Macroeconomics).
  • One curriculum across instructors: created and maintained the common Principles of Macroeconomics curriculum used by every instructor of the course at the University of Minnesota, as many as eight in a semester.
  • Pedagogy and online teaching: Pedagogy Fellow, then Associate Fellow, Chicago Center for Teaching and Learning; graded online assignments in Gradescope and trained colleagues to use it; contributed online-teaching methods and materials to the University of Chicago economics department.
